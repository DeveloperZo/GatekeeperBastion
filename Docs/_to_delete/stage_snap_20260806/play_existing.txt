A first-person wave-defense game about a door you cannot afford to lose.

Your ship latches onto a dormant alien derelict and starts draining it for salvage. The
umbilical back to your ship ends in a **Hatch**, and the Hatch is the only thing between
you and a dead run. Everything aboard the derelict wakes up and comes for it.

You are alone in a corridor with a gun, a door behind you, and a dial that decides how
hard the next few minutes are going to be.

## What a run is

You embark from your own ship. You arrive already tethered — there is no landing sequence
and no briefing. The derelict is quiet.

**It stays quiet until you decide otherwise.** There is no wave timer. Nothing spawns
until you pull the siphon draw, and that means the pause between waves is yours: repair
the Hatch, walk the room, pick your ground, or stand at the console and think. A game
that spawns on a clock is telling you when to be ready. This one asks.

Then you pull the draw and they come. Not in a stream — in **groups**, with a gap between
them, which is the difference between a fight you can read and a wall you cannot. Kill
them and the money is yours immediately, the instant each one drops. Clear the wave and
the quiet comes back.

Five waves. Clear them all and you have stripped the derelict.

## The wager

Before each wave you set the **siphon dial**. Higher pays more and sends more bodies.

It is deliberately a small, honest wager. The dial does not make boarders tougher, does
not tighten their spacing, and does not add a single extra Hunter or Ram — every body it
adds is the basic kind. You are betting that you can kill more of the same thing, not
that you can survive a different fight. And you set it in the quiet: once a wave is live
the dial locks, so it is a decision you commit to rather than one you back out of.

## The three things that come at you

They are not tiers of the same enemy. Each one is defined by something it **refuses** to
do, and that refusal is the whole design.

A **Default** marches on the Hatch and ignores you — until you hurt it, and then it comes
for you instead. It is the one that can be pulled off the door, and pulling it is a
choice with a cost.

A **Hunter** will never touch the Hatch. It wants you, it is faster than your walk and
slower than your sprint, and it dies quickly. It is the reason you cannot simply park in
front of the door and hold the trigger.

A **Ram** will never touch you. You can empty a clip into its back and it will not turn
around, because it does not care about you at all — it is walking to the door, and if it
gets there it takes a serious bite out of it. It is slow. That slowness is the offer: you
have time to deal with it, if you spend that time on it.

So the room asks three questions at once, and the answer to each makes the other two
harder. That is the game.

## Losing, and what losing costs

There are exactly two ways a run ends badly: **you die, or the Hatch dies.** There is no
bail-out, no escape lever, no cutting the tether early, no respawn. Once you are in, the
only exits are winning and losing.

But losing is cheap. **Every kill you banked stays banked.** Dying takes nothing back and
clearing takes nothing extra — the two endings differ in what the screen says and which
counter ticks, not in what you keep. A run that fell apart on wave two still paid for the
upgrade that gets you through wave two next time.

That is the deal the game makes with you: the runs are dangerous, and the failure is not.

## Between runs

You walk your own ship. Four consoles, and each owns exactly one subject so you always
know where a thing is bought.

**ARMORY** owns the weapon in your hands — the weapons themselves, and the upgrade Tiers
for whichever one you are carrying. Upgrades are *per weapon*, so pouring salvage into a
gun is a commitment to that gun, and buying a new one starts that investment over. Your
Primary is chosen here and fixed for the run; you decide what kind of fight you are
walking into before you walk into it.

**CREW QUARTERS** owns you: how much you can take, how far you can sprint, how fast you
repair, how much you earn, and your only source of critical hits.

**FABRICATION** owns the ship: how much the Hatch can take, and how quickly it comes back
when you hold the repair.

**MAP** owns where you go, and is where you commit to going there.

The first weapon is free, and it is still bought at the ARMORY — because the first thing
the game needs to teach you is that weapons come from that console.

## Where it is going

Two to four players on one hull. The whole thing is built for it already: the rules run
in a layer with no rendering in it, every object knows whether it would be owned by the
host or the player, and every change to the world goes through a single command path. The
co-op is not built. Nothing in the design is waiting on a rewrite to allow it.

## What it looks like right now

Gray boxes. No art pass, no audio pass, no menus beyond what the loop needs. The fight is
being made worth looking at before anyone makes it look like anything.

## Weapons

You carry one **Primary** and one **Secondary** at once, plus an empty melee slot.
The Primary is chosen at the ARMORY before you embark and is fixed for the run;
switching in the derelict swaps between the slots you already carry, never between
owned weapons. Each slot keeps its own clip, so switching is not a free reload.

| Weapon | Slot | Cost | Damage | Shots/s | Clip | Reload | Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **MK-1 Sidearm** | Secondary | 0 | 14 | 3 | 8 | 1.4 s | 45 m | free — always affordable |
| **BR-12 Shotgun** | Primary | 900 | 6 ×7 | 1.2 | 6 | 2.6 s | 14 m | 7 pellets, 7° spread; falls off past 8 m |
| **MR-35 Rifle** | Primary | 1,200 | 32 | 1.6 | 10 | 2.4 s | 80 m | pierces 2 |

### Tiers — what upgrading a weapon buys

Upgrades are **per weapon**, not global. Damage rises ×1.25 a Tier and clip ×1.15; fire rate never changes, because cadence is how a weapon reads. Tiers unlock
after your first death. Effective damage per second, every pellet connecting:

| Weapon | T0 | T1 | T2 | T3 | T4 | T5 | T6 | T7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MK-1 Sidearm | 28 | 36 | 47 | 61 | 79 | 102 | 131 | 167 |
| BR-12 Shotgun | 33 | 44 | 57 | 73 | 94 | 122 | 157 | 201 |
| MR-35 Rifle | 37 | 48 | 62 | 80 | 102 | 131 | 167 | 214 |

Each Tier costs 400 / 600 / 800 / 1,000 / 1,200 / 1,400 / 1,600 salvage — **7,000** to take one weapon all the way.

## What comes aboard

Three kinds, and each is defined by what it **refuses** to do. Every kind swings on
the same 0.7 s clock — they differ in what they hit, never in how often.

| Kind | HP | Hits you for | Hits the hatch for | Speed | Refuses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Default** | 30 | 6 | 4 | 3.2 → 5.6 m/s when provoked | nothing — it will march, and it will turn on you if you hurt it |
| **Hunter** | 20 | 6 | — | 6.5 m/s | to touch the hatch; it only ever wants you |
| **Ram** | 120 | — | 20 | 2.2 m/s | to notice you at all, even while you shoot it |

HP above is the base line. Later waves multiply it (see the wave table).

## The waves

**5 waves to a run**, and none of them start until you pull the
siphon draw. There is no timer: the quiet between waves lasts exactly as long as you
want it to. Boarders arrive in groups, not a stream.

At the dial's lowest setting:

| Wave | Bodies | Default | Hunter | Ram | HP × | Arrive in groups of | Every |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 18 | 18 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 3 | 4.2 s |
| 2 | 30 | 26 | 4 | 0 | 1.4 | 4 | 4 s |
| 3 | 42 | 34 | 6 | 2 | 1.8 | 5 | 3.8 s |
| 4 | 54 | 40 | 10 | 4 | 2.2 | 6 | 3.6 s |
| 5 | 66 | 47 | 12 | 7 | 2.6 | 7 | 3.4 s |

Hunters and Rams are on a fixed schedule — the dial never adds more of them.

## The siphon dial — the wager

Set between waves, locked once a wave is live. It pays more and sends more, and the
extra bodies are **all Defaults** — it cannot conjure a sixth Ram. It changes nothing
else: no extra health, no tighter spacing, no larger groups.

| Dial | You earn | Wave 1 sends | Wave 5 sends |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | ×1.00 | 18 | 66 |
| 1 | ×1.10 | 20 | 73 |
| 2 | ×1.15 | 21 | 76 |
| 3 | ×1.25 | 23 | 83 |
| 4 | ×1.35 | 24 | 89 |
| 5 | ×1.50 | 27 | 99 |

## Salvage

**Boarders are worth money and the money is yours the instant they die.** Not at the
end of the wave, not if you survive — the moment they drop. Dying takes none of it
back, and clearing the derelict adds no bonus. A run you lose still paid you.

A kill pays `25` at Scavenging 0, up to `31.25` at 5, then multiplied by the dial.
Nothing pays per second, and the quiet between waves earns nothing at all.

The hatch is what you are defending: **300 HP** at Hatch Armor 0, up to **375**. Hold `E` near it to repair.
If it falls, the run ends. If you fall, the run ends. There is no other way out —
no bail-out, no escape lever, no respawn.

## The ship — where salvage goes

Between runs you walk your own ship. Four consoles, each owning one subject.

| Console | Owns | Sells |
|---|---|---|
| **ARMORY** | the weapon in your hands | weapons, and Tiers for whichever you carry |
| **CREW QUARTERS** | you | Vitality, Stamina, Engineering, Scavenging, Luck |
| **FABRICATION** | the ship | Hatch Armor, Repair Rig |
| **MAP** | where you go | the destination, and embarking |

Attribute and ship tracks run 0–5 at +5% a level, costing 200 × 2^level — 200 / 400 / 800 / 1,600 / 3,200.

| Track | Level 0 | Level 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Vitality — your HP | 100 | 125 |
| Stamina — sprint pool | 100 | 125 |
| Engineering — repair speed | ×1 | ×1.25 |
| Scavenging — salvage per kill | 25 | 31.25 |
| Luck — crit chance (double damage) | 0% | 25% |
| Hatch Armor — hatch HP | 300 | 375 |
| Repair Rig — repair rate | 8 HP/s | 10 HP/s |

Luck is the only source of critical hits in the game, on purpose: two sources would
multiply against each other and the numbers on the panel would stop meaning anything.

**Attributes are never locked.** They are the answer to dying, so you can buy them
before you have died. Weapon Tiers unlock after your first death.

## The split, and why it holds

The manual is generated by `Tools/docgen/run.sh`, which calls the same code the game runs
— the binding list, the weapon catalog, the wave curve, the dial, the upgrade tracks. A
hand edit to it is lost on the next run, which is the point.

This exists because the hand-maintained versions had drifted three separate ways at once
by 2026-08-04: the root `README.md` listed a siphon dial on the number row that had moved
to `[` and `]`, `Docs/01_Game.md` sold a weapon the catalog no longer contained, and
`Docs/05_Decisions.md` recorded a five-wave retune as already shipped while
`WaveDirector` still said three. Three documents, three different games, none of them the
one in the repo.

So the rule is: **anything countable is generated; anything arguable is written.** A
weapon's damage is countable and belongs in the manual. Why a Ram refuses to notice you
is arguable and belongs in `The-Game.md`. If you find yourself hand-typing a number into
either file, it belongs in the generator instead.

`Tools/docgen/run.sh --check` fails if the committed manual no longer matches the build.
